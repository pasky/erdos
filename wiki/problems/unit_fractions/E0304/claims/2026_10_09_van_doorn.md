**Claim.** For integers $1\le a<b$ let $N(a,b)$ be the least number of
distinct unit fractions with denominators above $1$ whose sum is $a/b$, and
$N(b)=\max_{1\le a<b}N(a,b)$, the extremal function of
[[problems/unit_fractions/E0304/_index|Problem 304]]. Then

$$
N(b)\le\Bigl(\frac{16}{\log2}+o(1)\Bigr)\log\log b\qquad(b\to\infty),
$$

so $N(b)\ll\log\log b$ and the problem's question is answered yes. With
Erdős's lower bound of 1950, recorded on
[[problems/unit_fractions/E0304/claims/1950_01_01_erdos|its claim page]],
$N(b)\asymp\log\log b$. This is Theorem 2 (p. 2) of Wouter van Doorn,
*Short Egyptian fractions*, a six-page write-up added on 9 October 2026
(UTC, the claim's date) to his GitHub repository Woett/Mathematical-shorts at
the commit linked above. The theorem states that every fraction
$a/b\in(0,1)$ is a sum of at most that many distinct unit fractions. The
bound is uniform in $a$, and no denominator is $1$ since $a/b<1$. The
abstract calls the write-up a human-written exposition and simplification of
a proof recently posted by OpenAI, the release manuscript recorded on
[[problems/unit_fractions/E0304/claims/2026_09_25_openai|its claim page]].

The proof has three steps. First, greedy steps keep the denominator
unreduced, $b_i=bx_1\cdots x_i$, and stop when the remainder vanishes or
$b_r\ge n$. Here $n=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,t)^2$ with $t$ about
$\log b(\log\log b)^2/(2c)$. The remainder after $i$ steps is at most
$2^{-2^{i-1}}$, so this uses at most $(1/\log2+o(1))\log\log b$ unit
fractions (p. 2). Second, Lemma 3 (p. 3) says that $b_r$ has a divisor in
$[\mu,b\mu]$ for every real $1\le\mu\le b_r$. Repeated division by such
divisors writes $a_rn=d_1g_1+\cdots+d_sg_s$ with $d_j\mid b_r$,
$1\le g_j\le B=\exp(c\log n/\log\log n)$ and
$s\le(1/c+o(1))\log\log b$ (pp. 3--4). Third, Lemma 1 (p. 2, proved on
pp. 5--6) says that for $0<c<\tfrac23\log2$ and large $t$, every positive
integer up to $B$ is a sum of at most $10$ fractions $e/f$ with $e\mid n$.
So each $d_jg_j/(b_rn)$ splits into at most $10$ unit fractions, and the
total is at most $(1/\log2+10/c+o(1))\log\log b$. This tends to $16/\log2$
as $c$ tends to $\tfrac23\log2$. By Takenouchi's theorem of 1921, as the
write-up states it (p. 4), the unit fractions can then be made distinct
with the same number of terms. The proof of Lemma 1 writes each large odd
$m\le B$ as a sum of three primes by Vinogradov's theorem. The three primes
avoid the few primes $p$ modulo which the divisors of $P$, the product of
the first $2+\lceil\frac34\log_2m\rceil$ primes, occupy fewer than $p^{3/4}$
nonzero classes. Modulo each of the three primes, Lemma 4 (pp. 4--5, a
Cauchy–Schwarz count) writes $-1$ as $u_1v_1+u_2v_2$ with $u_i,v_i\mid P$.
So each prime is a sum of three fractions whose numerators divide $P^2$,
which divides $n$. An even $m$ adds the fraction $1/1$.

**Submission note.** Posted to the site's forum by Wouter van Doorn (account
Woett) on 10 October 2026, in the comment thread of the curator's blog post
"Erdős problems in the OpenAI proofs":

> **Human-generated exposition**
>
> With the help of ChatGPT I have substantially simplified OpenAI's solution to
> #304. You can find my write-up here, proving that a fraction
> $\frac{a}{b} \in (0, 1)$ can be written as the sum of at most
> $\left(\frac{16}{\log 2} + o(1) \right) \log \log b$ distinct unit fractions.
>
> **Optimality**
>
> The constant of $\frac{16}{\log 2} \approx 23.08$ is pretty good (compare
> e.g. with the constant $\frac{257}{\log 2} \approx 370.77$ that appears in
> the OpenAI paper), but not optimal. Indeed, with more effort (and by relying
> on various results from the literature on the distribution of prime numbers)
> one can lower it to $\frac{7}{\log 2} \approx 10.10$, and perhaps further
> still. I tried to keep the write-up as simple as possible, however, so I
> decided that $\frac{16}{\log 2}$ is good enough for now.
>
> **Applications**
>
> In the proof we start with a greedy expansion for the first
> $\left(\frac{1}{\log 2} + o(1) \right) \log \log b$ terms. Now, it is
> trivial to do this greedy start but with taking $\frac{1}{b+1}$ instead of
> $\frac{1}{b}$ if the latter would be correct according to the greedy
> strategy. With this slight modification, if we started with the fraction
> $\frac{b-1}{b}$, then we can simply add $\frac{1}{b}$ at the end to obtain a
> decomposition of $1$ as a sum of distinct unit fractions that contains
> $\frac{1}{b}$, which is what we aim for in #293. Long story short, in the
> notation of #293, the linked proof immediately gives $v(k) > e^{e^{ck}}$,
> which in turn implies $F(k) > e^{e^{ck}}$ for #148 as well, both of which
> are optimal up to the value of $c$. Three solutions for the price of one!
>
> **Some words on the OpenAI paper**
>
> It's.. Bizarre. In the first 5 sections of the paper they prove that
> $\ll \log \log b$ unit fractions suffice. The proof takes about 20 pages and
> seems quite complicated; I haven't even tried to understand any of it.
> Section 6 and 7 then apply the proven results on #304 to #148 and #293
> respectively. So far so sensible (despite the apparent difficulty).
>
> But then, Section 8 aims to resolve #293 with the explicit constant of
> $\frac{257}{\log 2}$. This is done by a completely different and
> conceptually much easier argument! For some reason #304 and #148 are not
> even mentioned in this section, even though this proof works just as fine
> for either. And seemingly, the formalisations of the solutions for the
> latter two problems go through the original much more difficult proof. The
> formalisation of #293 on the other hand, does use this easier proof. The
> only problem is, this easier proof relies on Vinogradov's theorem on the odd
> Goldbach conjecture, which is probably hard to formalise. So the
> formalisation actually modifies the easy proof in a way as to avoid
> Vinogradov's theorem.
>
> In any case, the write-up I linked gives (a simplification of) the
> relatively easy Vinogradov-reliant proof, with the first 7 sections of the
> OpenAI paper completely irrelevant and ignored.

**Depends on.** Nothing in this wiki. The write-up proves its lemmas itself.
Its outside inputs are the prime number theorem, Vinogradov's three-prime
theorem (cited through Kumchev and Tolev's survey) and Takenouchi's 1921
theorem on making a sum of unit fractions distinct. The write-up asserts
without derivation that every large odd $m$ has at least $m/(20\log^2m)$
pairwise disjoint representations as a sum of three primes. The proof needs
only one representation that avoids the exceptional primes, and the
release's proof of its Lemma 8.2 obtains one by counting ordered triples.
The write-up does not use the release's theorems.

**Relation to other claims.** Section 8 of the release's manuscript proves,
for Problem 293, that every large $m$ is a denominator in a representation
of $1$ by at most $(257/\log2+\varepsilon)\log\log m$ distinct unit
fractions (Proposition 8.1). Its two main lemmas are a supply of fractions
whose numerators divide a fixed integer, resting on the three-prime theorem
(Lemma 8.2), and the divisor property of the unreduced greedy denominator
(Lemma 8.3), as
[[../library/unit_fractions/openai_2026_short_egyptian_fractions/_index|the release's source card]]
records. The write-up's Lemmas 1 and 3 play the same roles. Its Lemma 1
writes each prime with three fractions where Lemma 8.2 uses five, so it
needs at most ten summands where Lemma 8.2 needs sixteen. It applies the
construction to $a/b$ directly and does not use Sections 2--7 of the
manuscript. The manuscript's Theorem 1.1, the accepted claim, leaves its
constant unspecified. This is a different result from
[[problems/unit_fractions/E0304/claims/2026_09_16_van_doorn|van Doorn and GPT-6 Astra Pro's note of 16 September 2026]],
whose bound $2c_0(\log\log b)^2$ came from practical numbers; this bound
implies it.

**AI use.** The write-up names no AI system and calls itself human-written.
The forum post announcing it, under the heading "Human-generated
exposition", says that the simplification was made with the help of
ChatGPT. ChatGPT is credited only with assistance, so it is not listed as
an author.

**Not proved in the write-up.** Two statements of the forum post are not in
the write-up. The first is that the constant can be lowered to $7/\log2$
with more effort, using results on the distribution of primes; no argument
is given. The second is that a greedy start that never takes $1/b$, applied
to $(b-1)/b$, gives $v(k)>e^{e^{ck}}$ for
[[problems/unit_fractions/E0293/_index|Problem 293]] and hence
$F(k)>e^{e^{ck}}$ for [[problems/unit_fractions/E0148/_index|Problem 148]].
The write-up mentions neither problem, so these consequences are sketches in
the post. They are noted on those problem pages and have no claim pages.
Lower bounds of this form are already accepted there, from the release's
Corollaries 1.3 and 1.2.

**Standing.** Claimed. The write-up is a PDF on GitHub, not on arXiv and not
refereed, with no Lean formalization, and no outside review is known. Here
the proofs of Theorem 2 and Lemmas 1, 3 and 4 were read step by step against
the problem's statement, and no gap was found. That reading takes as given
the prime number theorem, Takenouchi's theorem and the asserted count of
disjoint three-prime representations. It is the corpus's own reading, not an
independent review, and it supplies no evidence. The curator replied to the
post on 10 October 2026 with thanks, which is commentary, not acceptance. The
site labeled the problem OPEN (page last edited 29 December 2025). The
problem's standing rests on
[[problems/unit_fractions/E0304/claims/2026_09_25_openai|the release's accepted theorem]];
if accepted, this claim would give a second proof, with an explicit
constant.
