# problems/discrete_geometry/E1088/claims

[[problems/discrete_geometry/E1088/_index|..]]

[[problems/discrete_geometry/E1088/claims/1947_04_01_erdos_kelly|1947_04_01_erdos_kelly]]: The solution of Monthly problem E735, posed by Erdős and solved by Kelly,
shows that no seven points of the plane have every triple isosceles, while a
regular pentagon with its center does, so $f_2(3)=7$.

[[problems/discrete_geometry/E1088/claims/1962_01_01_croft|1962_01_01_croft]]: Croft proves that every nine points of $\mathbb{R}^3$ contain three at
pairwise distinct distances. Kelly's eight-point set has no such three points,
so $f_3(3)=9$.

[[problems/discrete_geometry/E1088/claims/1975_01_01_komlos_sulyok_szemeredi|1975_01_01_komlos_sulyok_szemeredi]]: Komlós, Sulyok and Szemerédi show that every set of $N$ integers contains a
Sidon subset of size at least $cN^{1/2}$, so $f_1(n)\le Cn^2$. With the
Erdős–Turán bound, this fixes the order $n^2$ of the one-dimensional case.

[[problems/discrete_geometry/E1088/claims/1984_01_01_blokhuis|1984_01_01_blokhuis]]: Blokhuis shows that an isosceles set in $\mathbb{R}^d$ has at most
$(d+1)(d+2)/2$ points, so $f_d(3)\le(d+1)(d+2)/2+1$, which is polynomial in
$d$, and the question has answer yes for $n=3$. The source is a CWI Tract, not
a journal publication.

[[problems/discrete_geometry/E1088/claims/2009_11_24_ionin|2009_11_24_ionin]]: Ionin determines the largest isosceles sets in $\mathbb{R}^d$ for $d\le8$,
with 3, 6, 8, 11, 17, 28, 30 and 45 points, so $f_d(3)$ is 4, 7, 9, 12, 18,
29, 31 and 46 for $d=1,\dots,8$.
