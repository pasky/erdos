**Claim.** There is an infinite sequence $A=\{n_1<n_2<\cdots\}$ of positive
integers with

$$
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1N\sum_{k\le N}\frac{\phi_A(k)}{n_k}=0,
$$

where $\phi_A(k)$ counts the $1\le m\le n_k$ whose fraction $m/n_k$ in lowest
terms has a denominator different from every earlier $n_j$. This answers
[[problems/irrationality/E1000/_index|Problem 1000]] yes, against the
expectation Erdős recorded in 1964, when he could show only that
$\phi_A(k)/n_k$ cannot tend to zero and that a zero lower limit forces an
upper limit of one. Cassels had shown in 1950 that the lower limit of the
average can be zero for his count, which excludes every $m$ whose reduced
denominator divides an earlier term (card
[[../library/irrationality/cassels_1950_some_metrical_theorems_diophantine_approximation_i/_index|cassels_1950]]);
the question asks for the full limit. The result is in J. A. Haight, Metric
Diophantine Approximation and Related Topics, PhD thesis, University of
London, Westfield College, dated February 1971 on its title page (the
erdosproblems.com entry [Ha] gives no year). The erdosproblems.com thread
describes the construction as Cassels's construction combined with a
multiplication of the sequence, and the Lean file below builds the sequence from
blocks of multiples of a large prime by the divisors of a primorial.

The claim is stated in erdosproblems.com's count, which never falls below
Cassels's, the count of Erdős's question [Er64b, p. 59], so it also answers that
question. The thread's account rests on the average being unchanged when a
sequence is multiplied by a constant, which holds for Cassels's count but not
for erdosproblems.com's (for the sequence $1,2$ the second ratio is $1/2$ under
both counts; for $2,4$ it is $1/2$ under Cassels's and $3/4$ under
erdosproblems.com's); the erdosproblems.com version is the one the Lean file
below proves, by its own construction.

**Acceptance.** The `reviewed` evidence is the documented acceptance by
erdosproblems.com. Its page carried the label PROVED (LEAN), last edited
2025-12-05, and its curator, Thomas Bloom, credits Haight with the proof that
such a sequence exists. The thread (the second `discussion` link) carries the
posts of 2025-12-04 that identified the thesis (its Chapter 2) as the solution.
Those posts also said that Erdős mentions the solution on p. 5 of his 1975 paper
*Problems and results on diophantine approximations (II)*, and noted that
ChatGPT helped locate the reference. No refereed journal version is known. The
thesis is a doctoral dissertation, and no `refereed` evidence is listed.

**Formalization.** A third party formalized the solution: `thm_main` in
`src/v4.29.1/ErdosProblems/Erdos1000.lean` of
https://github.com/plby/lean-proofs, pinned above at the commit of
2026-06-24, the file's version of that date (the proof entered the
repository on 2025-12-28). The file declares itself a formalization of a
solution to the problem, names Haight and ChatGPT as informal
authors and Aristotle and Boris Alexeev as formal authors,
so it is a link on this page rather than an independent claim. Its theorem
gives a strictly increasing $n:\mathbb N\to\mathbb N$ with
$\frac1N\sum_{k=1}^N\phi_A(k)/n_k\to0$, with $\phi_A$ defined by the
problem's condition $n_k/\gcd(m,n_k)\ne n_j$; it imports only Mathlib and
contains no `sorry`. The formal-conjectures catalog (the `record` link,
pinned to the commit of 2026-09-18 that last touched its file) tags its own
statement `erdos_1000` as `research solved` and cites this file as the
formal proof; its definition uses non-divisibility
$n_k/\gcd(m,n_k)\nmid n_j$, Cassels's count, to which the formal-conjectures
catalog corrected its earlier inequality on 2026-09-13; since that count never
exceeds erdosproblems.com's, the linked proof of the erdosproblems.com version
implies the formal-conjectures statement. This repository has not built the
file, printed its axioms or audited its definitions against the problem, so the
claim carries no `formalized` evidence and erdosproblems.com's "(LEAN)"
qualifier is reported, not warranted, here.
