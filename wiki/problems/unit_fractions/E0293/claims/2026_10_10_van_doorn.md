***

**Claim.** In a post of 10 October 2026 on the erdosproblems.com forum, Wouter
van Doorn announced his write-up *Short Egyptian fractions*, recorded on
[[problems/unit_fractions/E0304/claims/2026_10_09_van_doorn|Problem 304's claim page]],
and stated that in the notation of
[[problems/unit_fractions/E0293/_index|Problem 293]] "the linked proof
immediately gives $v(k) > e^{e^{ck}}$", which he called optimal up to the
value of $c$. The post gives no value of $c$ and no range of $k$. Its argument
is a sketch. The write-up's proof that a fraction $a/b\in(0,1)$ is a sum of
at most $(16/\log2+o(1))\log\log b$ distinct unit fractions begins with about
$(1/\log2+o(1))\log\log b$ greedy steps, and the post says that these can be
modified to take $1/(b+1)$ wherever the greedy choice would be $1/b$. Started
from $(b-1)/b$, the expansion would then avoid $1/b$, and adding $1/b$ would
give a representation of $1$ by distinct unit fractions that contains $1/b$.
The post does not say how the later steps of the proof, among them the final
use of Takenouchi's theorem to make the unit fractions distinct, are kept from
producing the denominator $b$ (an observation made here). The passage from a
representation of length at most $k$ to one of length exactly $k$ is van
Doorn and Tang's nesting $D_k\subseteq D_{k+1}$ (their Lemma 2.1), the
converse direction of the link in
[[../library/unit_fractions/doorn_2025_smallest_denominator_not_contained_unit_fraction/section_3|Section 3 of their paper]];
the post leaves it implicit. The post adds that the bound implies
$F(k)>e^{e^{ck}}$ for Problem 148, which is recorded on
[[problems/unit_fractions/E0148/claims/2026_10_10_van_doorn|Problem 148's claim page]].

**Submission note.** Posted to the erdosproblems.com forum by Wouter van Doorn
(account Woett) on 10 October 2026, in the comment thread of the curator's blog
post "Erdős problems in the OpenAI proofs":

> **Human-generated exposition**
>
> With the help of ChatGPT I have substantially simplified OpenAI's solution to
> #304. You can find my write-up here, proving that a fraction
> $\frac{a}{b} \in (0, 1)$ can be written as the sum of at most
> $\left(\frac{16}{\log 2} + o(1) \right) \log \log b$ distinct unit fractions.
>
> **Optimality**
>
> The constant of $\frac{16}{\log 2} \approx 23.08$ is pretty good (compare
> e.g. with the constant $\frac{257}{\log 2} \approx 370.77$ that appears in
> the OpenAI paper), but not optimal. Indeed, with more effort (and by relying
> on various results from the literature on the distribution of prime numbers)
> one can lower it to $\frac{7}{\log 2} \approx 10.10$, and perhaps further
> still. I tried to keep the write-up as simple as possible, however, so I
> decided that $\frac{16}{\log 2}$ is good enough for now.
>
> **Applications**
>
> In the proof we start with a greedy expansion for the first
> $\left(\frac{1}{\log 2} + o(1) \right) \log \log b$ terms. Now, it is
> trivial to do this greedy start but with taking $\frac{1}{b+1}$ instead of
> $\frac{1}{b}$ if the latter would be correct according to the greedy
> strategy. With this slight modification, if we started with the fraction
> $\frac{b-1}{b}$, then we can simply add $\frac{1}{b}$ at the end to obtain a
> decomposition of $1$ as a sum of distinct unit fractions that contains
> $\frac{1}{b}$, which is what we aim for in #293. Long story short, in the
> notation of #293, the linked proof immediately gives $v(k) > e^{e^{ck}}$,
> which in turn implies $F(k) > e^{e^{ck}}$ for #148 as well, both of which
> are optimal up to the value of $c$. Three solutions for the price of one!
>
> **Some words on the OpenAI paper**
>
> It's.. Bizarre. In the first 5 sections of the paper they prove that
> $\ll \log \log b$ unit fractions suffice. The proof takes about 20 pages and
> seems quite complicated; I haven't even tried to understand any of it.
> Section 6 and 7 then apply the proven results on #304 to #148 and #293
> respectively. So far so sensible (despite the apparent difficulty).
>
> But then, Section 8 aims to resolve #293 with the explicit constant of
> $\frac{257}{\log 2}$. This is done by a completely different and
> conceptually much easier argument! For some reason #304 and #148 are not
> even mentioned in this section, even though this proof works just as fine
> for either. And seemingly, the formalisations of the solutions for the
> latter two problems go through the original much more difficult proof. The
> formalisation of #293 on the other hand, does use this easier proof. The
> only problem is, this easier proof relies on Vinogradov's theorem on the odd
> Goldbach conjecture, which is probably hard to formalise. So the
> formalisation actually modifies the easy proof in a way as to avoid
> Vinogradov's theorem.
>
> In any case, the write-up I linked gives (a simplification of) the
> relatively easy Vinogradov-reliant proof, with the first 7 sections of the
> OpenAI paper completely irrelevant and ignored.

**Covers.** A doubly exponential lower bound $v(k)>e^{e^{ck}}$ for some
$c>0$, the form of the lower bound of
[[problems/unit_fractions/E0293/claims/2026_09_25_openai|the release's accepted Corollary 1.3]],
which gives $v(k)\ge e^{e^{k/600}}$ for all large $k$ and
$\liminf\log\log v(k)/k\ge\log2/257$. With no value of $c$, the claim does not
improve that slope. It says nothing about the upper bound, and the growth
question stays open.

**Depends on.**
[[problems/unit_fractions/E0304/claims/2026_10_09_van_doorn|Van Doorn's write-up on Problem 304]],
whose proof the post would modify; the write-up itself mentions neither
Problem 293 nor Problem 148.

**Standing.** Rejected: the claim does not improve the best known bound.
Lower bounds of this form already rest on the release's accepted Corollary
1.3, and with no value of $c$ the claim adds nothing to it. The claim is also
a sketch in a forum post: neither the modified greedy start nor the deduction
of the bound on $v(k)$ is written out in the write-up or elsewhere, and the
corpus has not checked it. No review exists. The curator replied to the post
on 10 October 2026 with thanks, which is commentary, not acceptance. Bloom's
catalog labeled the problem OPEN (page last edited 29 December 2025).
