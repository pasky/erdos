## Restatement

For each integer $r\geq2$, put $n=r-1$ and choose a set $S$ of size $2n$.
Let $\mathcal P$ consist of all unordered partitions
$\{A,S\setminus A\}$ with $|A|=n$. Attach a distinct vertex $x_P$ outside $S$
to each $P\in\mathcal P$. Define

$$
V_r=S\mathbin{\dot\cup}\{x_P:P\in\mathcal P\},
$$

$$
\mathcal E_r=
\{T\subseteq S:|T|=n+1\}
\;\cup\;
\{A\cup\{x_P\}:P\in\mathcal P,\ A\in P\}.
$$

The claim is that this finite hypergraph has edges of size $r$, any two
distinct edges intersect, every member of $V_r$ belongs to an edge, and its
chromatic number for the nonmonochromatic-edge convention is exactly three.
Moreover, writing $N_r=|V_r|$,

$$
N_r=2r-2+\frac12\binom{2r-2}{r-1},
\qquad
\lim_{\substack{r\to\infty\\r\in\mathbb Z}}\frac{N_r}{r^2}=+\infty.
$$

Consequently there are no constants $C>0$ and $r_0$ such that every admissible
hypergraph of every integer uniformity $r\geq r_0$ has at most $Cr^2$ vertices.
Here admissibility can mean the modified formulation, with exact chromatic
number three and all vertices incident, or either reading of the first
formulation: exact chromatic number three, or merely the existence of a proper
three-colouring. The constant is uniform in both $r$ and the hypergraph.

The equality for $N_r$ describes this construction. It is not an assertion of
maximality among all admissible hypergraphs, nor does it assert unbounded size
at one fixed value of $r$.

## Domain and boundary checks

The finite family of subsets of $S$ supplies a finite partition set. Distinct
labels outside $S$ can be supplied by a disjoint tagged set; no existence
theorem about hypergraphs is being assumed. The two halves of each partition
are disjoint and nonempty because $n\geq1$. Complementation does not fix a
half-set, and a half-set determines its unordered partition uniquely.
Both the family of edges inside $S$ and the pairs of edges indexed by
partitions are finite, so the vertex set and edge set are finite.

Every listed edge has $n+1=r$ elements. An edge inside $S$ cannot equal one
containing a new vertex. Edges assigned different new vertices cannot coincide,
and the two edges assigned to one partition are distinct. Thus the definition
also gives a simple hypergraph; no convention allowing repeated edges is
needed.

For any $s\in S$, there are $2n-1\geq n$ other points, so adjoining any $n$ of
them gives an edge inside $S$ containing $s$. Each $x_P$ belongs to both edges
indexed by $P$. Thus $V_r=\bigcup\mathcal E_r$, and the cardinality counts
incident vertices without padding by isolated labels.

The endpoint $r=2$ gives $S=\{a,b\}$, a single partition, and a single new
vertex $x$. The complete edge list is $\{a,b\}$, $\{a,x\}$, $\{b,x\}$.
It is the three-vertex triangle: all edges have size two and meet pairwise,
all vertices are incident, two colours force a monochromatic edge, and three
distinct colours work. The count is $2+\tfrac12\binom21=3$. At this endpoint
there are no two distinct edges inside $S$ and no two distinct partitions;
those cases of the general intersection argument are vacuous, not exceptions
to its conclusion. This explicit case is a boundary diagnostic, not a finite
substitute for the arbitrary-$r$ arguments.

## Weakest steps

### 1. Complementary halves and all edge-pair types

For subsets $U,W$ of $S$, counting their union gives

$$
|U\cap W|=|U|+|W|-|U\cup W|\geq |U|+|W|-2n.
$$

Two edges lying inside $S$ each have size $n+1$, so their intersection has
size at least two. An edge $T$ inside $S$ and a half-set $A$ have intersection
of size at least $(n+1)+n-2n=1$, proving intersection with
$A\cup\{x_P\}$ as well.

The two distinct edges indexed by the same partition have intersection exactly
$\{x_P\}$. It remains to try to separate edges indexed by distinct
partitions $P,Q$. Write their old parts as $A\in P$ and $B\in Q$.
Their new vertices are distinct and outside $S$, so their intersection is
precisely $A\cap B$. If this were empty, then
$B\subseteq S\setminus A$ and both sets would have size $n$. Therefore
$B=S\setminus A$, forcing

$$
Q=\{B,S\setminus B\}=\{S\setminus A,A\}=P,
$$

contrary to their being distinct. These cases exhaust pairs of edges. This
attack fails for every $n\geq1$; it does not assert a common vertex in all
edges, which the question does not require.

### 2. Exact chromatic number and the extension obstruction

Try any two-colouring of $V_r$, with colours red and blue. If either colour
occurs at least $n+1$ times on $S$, those vertices contain a monochromatic
edge inside $S$. Avoiding this requires at most $n$ old vertices of each
colour. Since all $2n$ old vertices receive a colour, the red class $R$ and
blue class $B$ must each have size $n$.

This balancing condition gives an actual index
$P_0=\{R,B\}\in\mathcal P$, not merely an abstract partition of the same
sizes. Giving $x_{P_0}$ red makes $R\cup\{x_{P_0}\}$ monochromatic; giving
it blue makes $B\cup\{x_{P_0}\}$ monochromatic. These are both edges in
the frozen definition and use the same new vertex. Hence no two-colouring
extends even this best possible colouring of $S$.

The obstruction is especially sharp: if $Q\ne P_0$, a half-set in $Q$
cannot be all red, since a red $n$-subset would have to be $R$ and would
determine $P_0$; the same holds for blue. Thus both old halves of $Q$ are
mixed, and either colour could be given to $x_Q$. When $n=1$, there is no
such $Q$. Even granting independent, unrestricted colour choices at all these
other new vertices cannot resolve the impossible choice at $x_{P_0}$.

For the separate upper bound of three colours, choose any partition
$S=A_0\mathbin{\dot\cup}B_0$ with both parts of size $n$. Colour $A_0$
with colour 1, $B_0$ with colour 2, and every new vertex with colour 3.
An $(n+1)$-subset of $S$ cannot lie in either old colour class, so it meets
both. Every edge with a new vertex contains colour 3 and also $n\geq1$ old
vertices, whose colours are 1 or 2. This verifies the colouring for all
partitions, not just the one chosen for the colour split. All three colours
occur. Together with the failure of every two-colouring, this proves exact
chromatic number three.

### 3. The count, its growth, and both consequence sentences

The map taking an $n$-subset $A$ to $\{A,S\setminus A\}$ has exactly two
preimages per partition: its two halves. They are distinct since they are
disjoint nonempty sets. Thus

$$
|\mathcal P|=\frac12\binom{2n}{n},
\qquad
N_r=2n+|\mathcal P|=2r-2+\frac12\binom{2r-2}{r-1}.
$$

The distinct new labels are outside $S$, so this is a disjoint-union count.
Counting ordered pairs instead would introduce a factor of two; the frozen
definition explicitly uses unordered partitions. This also explains why the
half-binomial expression is an integer.

To check the displayed growth estimate, the consecutive-coefficient identity

$$
\frac{\binom{2n}{k+1}}{\binom{2n}{k}}=
\frac{2n-k}{k+1}\qquad(0\leq k<2n)
$$

shows strict increase up to the central entry and strict decrease after it.
Counting all subsets of a $2n$-element set by their size gives
$\sum_{k=0}^{2n}\binom{2n}{k}=2^{2n}=4^n$. There are $2n+1$ entries, so
the maximum is at least their arithmetic mean. Therefore

$$
\frac{N_r}{r^2}\geq
\frac{\binom{2r-2}{r-1}}{2r^2}\geq
b_r:=\frac{4^{r-1}}{2(2r-1)r^2}>0.
$$

This is an exact finite counting inequality for arbitrary $n$, not a
probabilistic averaging heuristic. In the ratio's own coordinates,

$$
\frac{b_{r+1}}{b_r}=
4\frac{2r-1}{2r+1}\left(\frac{r}{r+1}\right)^2
\longrightarrow 4.
$$

An explicit version of the frozen argument's eventual doubling is available:
for every $r\geq5$, the two fractions are at least $9/11$ and $5/6$,
respectively. Consequently $b_{r+1}/b_r\geq25/11>2$, and for every integer
$k\geq0$,

$$
b_{5+k}\geq b_5\,2^k=\frac{128}{225}\,2^k\longrightarrow+\infty.
$$

This proves the claimed limit through integers. The threshold matters:
$b_5/b_4=448/225<2$. An assertion of doubling at every $r\geq2$ would
fail, but the frozen text asserts eventual doubling only.

Now suppose a uniform quadratic bound held, even only for $r\geq r_0$.
Choose $k$ with $5+k\geq r_0$ and $(128/225)2^k>C$; the graph
$G_{5+k}$ then has $N_{5+k}>C(5+k)^2$. Thus both the stated limit and its
negative answer to the uniform-bound question follow, with no exchange of
limits or hidden dependence of the constants on the graph.

## Strongest attack and retained failed attacks

The strongest attempted refutation sought a two-colouring by first using an
arbitrary balanced split of $S$, which removes every monochromatic edge inside
$S$, and then choosing the colours of all new vertices independently. This
could have broken the proof if one balanced split were absent from the
partition indices, if complementary halves used different new vertices, or if
one of the two edges were missing. The frozen definition has none of these
defects. The exact obstruction is $x_{\{R,B\}}$: its red choice violates
$R\cup\{x_{\{R,B\}}\}$ and its blue choice violates
$B\cup\{x_{\{R,B\}}\}$. Every other partition imposes no restriction
on its new vertex under this balanced split. This locates the unavoidable
failure after giving a proposed colouring all available freedom; unbalanced
splits already fail on an edge inside $S$. The attack therefore covers all
two-colourings symbolically, without a bounded search.

Other attempted failures and their exact resolutions are retained as follows:

- **Disjoint edges from different partitions.** Distinct new vertices offer
  no shared point. Disjoint old halves would have to be complements, which
  identifies their unordered partitions and contradicts distinctness. The
  other edge-pair types are covered in weakest step 1.
- **A degenerate endpoint or empty half-set.** The explicit $r=2$ triangle
  meets every clause. Nonemptiness needed for the colouring and count follows
  from $r-1\geq1$; no inference silently assumes $r\geq3$.
- **Double-counted or nonincident vertices.** Complement fibres have exactly
  two elements, labels are distinct and outside $S$, and every label is
  incident. The formula counts actual vertices, not ordered representations.
- **An asymptotic statement stronger than its estimate.** The exact quotient
  and $448/225<2$ test distinguish eventual from all-order doubling. The
  fixed threshold $r=5$ and positive geometric lower bound prove the limit
  actually asserted.
- **A conclusion lost on changing formulations.** The same graphs meet the
  stricter hypotheses and have a proper three-colouring. The class-containment
