### E1: block sieve and its five uses

Take a finite weighted population with nonnegative weights, a finite set
$\mathcal P$ of primes at most $z$, and a bad event $E_p$ for each of them.
For every subset $S\subset\mathcal P$, including the empty set, let
$d_S=\prod_{p\in S}p$ and suppose its intersection weight is
$Xg(d_S)+r(d_S)$, where $X\ge0$, $g(1)=1$, $g$ is multiplicative on the
squarefree products, and, for fixed $0<\eta_0<1$, $C\ge1$,

$$
0\le g(p)\le1-\eta_0,\qquad
\sum_{\substack{p\in\mathcal P\\w<p\le w^2}}g(p)\le C
\quad\hbox{for every }w>1.
$$

Let $\mathcal S$ be the weight avoiding all bad events and
$G=\prod_{p\in\mathcal P}(1-g(p))$. E1 asserts the existence of
$h_0,c_1,c_2$ depending only on $\eta_0,C$, such that for every even
$h\ge h_0$,

$$
|\mathcal S-XG|\le c_1e^{-h}XG+
 \sum_{\substack{d\le z^{4h+2}\\d\text{ a squarefree product of }\mathcal P}}
 |r(d)|,
$$

and at $h=2$,
$\mathcal S\le c_2XG+\sum_{d\le z^{10}}|r(d)|$.
It further asserts bounding polynomials $V_0\le\mathbf1_{\rm avoid}\le U$
with $U\ge0$ and $U-V_0\ge0$, each of $U,V_0,U-V_0$ having coefficients
in $\{-1,0,1\}$ and support $d_S\le z^{4h+1}$. Here $V_0$ denotes the
page's lower polynomial $V$, to distinguish it from the prime product above.
There is no explicit lower bound on $z$ in the frozen statement. The
large-$h$ conclusion is uniform when $h$ grows, not merely for a separate
fixed $h$ at each application.

The five prescribed instances are: integers in arbitrary real subintervals
and reduced residue classes modulo $r\le L^B$ at $z=W$,
$h=2\lceil T^2\rceil$; candidate integers satisfying $p\mid2u-1$ for a prime
$p\in(x^\gamma,x^{\gamma'}]$, sieved at the same $W,h$; the preliminary
candidate sieve at $z=x^{b_1}$,
$h=2\lfloor1/(40b_1)\rfloor$; ordinary rough integers in $(x,2x]$ at that
same cutoff and $h$; and the balanced lattice box at $h=2$,
$z=x^\theta$. The first has $g(p)=1/p$, the second and third have
$g(p)=1/(p-1)$ for odd primes, the fourth has $g(p)=1/p$, and the fifth
has $g(l)=\nu(l)/l^2\le(3l-2)/l^2\le7/9$ for odd $l$.
