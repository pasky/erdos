## Mathematical grading of the weakest steps

### Completion, minimum palette, and incidences

The report's first reconstruction preserves the direction needed for a
contradiction: if a list assignment is bad on $G$, it remains bad after all
cross-edges of a fixed bipartition are added. A coloring of that complete
bipartite graph would color $G$. No reverse implication is used. Adding those
edges changes neither the number of vertices nor any list.

On that fixed graph, the palette of any assignment has at most $4v$ colors.
Given a bad assignment, the attained bad palette sizes form a nonempty set of
integers and have a minimum $N$. Arbitrary labels cause no difficulty: palettes
may be relabeled, and the minimum concerns their sizes. This is not an
algorithm whose termination presupposes colorability.

The most consequential transfer was challenged at its exact hypothesis. If
colors $p,q$ never share a list, the quotient map identifying them is injective
on each list. Thus four distinct colors remain at every vertex. A coloring of
the quotient lists lifts uniquely within each original list. Equal lifted
colors at adjacent vertices would have equal images, contradicting the
quotient coloring. Hence the quotient is still bad and has one fewer palette
color, which contradicts minimality. Merging a cooccurring pair would fail the
list-size requirement, but that pair is not one the proof merges.

Every palette pair must therefore occur in a vertex list. Counting all six
pairs per list gives $\binom N2\le6v$. If color $x$ occurs in $t_x$ vertex
lists, those occurrences offer at most $3t_x$ distinct partners. All $N-1$
partners are required, so

$$
t_x\ge\left\lceil\frac{N-1}{3}\right\rceil,
\qquad
4v=\sum_x t_x
\ge N\left\lceil\frac{N-1}{3}\right\rceil.
$$

Repeated lists and repeated partners cannot reverse either inequality. The
report expands precisely the fixed-color count asserted in the frozen proof;
it does not supply a new combinatorial premise in place of it.

### Covering and symmetrization

For an $\ell$-subset $T$ of the palette, meeting every left list and
containing no right list permits choices from $T$ on the left and its
complement on the right. The palettes are disjoint and there are no edges
within a part. This is a proved sufficient condition, not a claim that an
arbitrary coloring must have a prescribed palette size.

In a bad assignment every $T$ therefore misses a left list or contains a right
list. Put

$$
X=\binom{N-4}{\ell},\qquad
Y=\binom{N-4}{\ell-4},\qquad
C=\binom N\ell.
$$

Counting pairs of a subset and an obstructing vertex gives $aX+cY\ge C$.
Overlapping obstructions increase this incidence count; they do not require
disjointness. Replacing $\ell$ by $N-\ell$ interchanges $X,Y$ and leaves
$C$ unchanged, giving $aY+cX\ge C$. Adding yields
$v(X+Y)\ge2C$ without $a=c$.

For nonempty bad assignments $N\ge4$, so the upper binomial arguments are
nonnegative. A negative lower argument or one exceeding the upper argument
counts no subsets. In particular, at $N=7,\ell=3$ the two unsymmetrized
inequalities are $a\ge35$ and $c\ge35$. At an even midpoint the argument
adds an inequality to itself, which is valid. The report's complement identities
and multiplicity explanation expose routine counting in the frozen argument;
no additional covering theorem is needed.

## Least-order reconstruction: explicit ruling

The lower-bound implication and nonemptiness are different obligations. From
the order-25 theorem, every graph in the class with list chromatic number
greater than four has integer order at least 26. That implication alone does
not prove that such a graph exists. A finite minimum requires nonemptiness,
and the frozen proof does not exhibit it. The report expressly acknowledges
that distinction and supplies an additional elementary construction.

The construction already in the graded report is valid. Four left vertices
receive pairwise disjoint four-element lists $U_1,\ldots,U_4$. There is a
right vertex for each of the $4^4=256$ tuples of left choices, adjacent to
every left vertex, with the four chosen colors as its list. Disjointness makes
each right list a four-element set. For any coloring of the left side, its
tuple-indexed right vertex has every available color forbidden by a neighbor.
Thus this finite simple bipartite graph has a bad four-list assignment.

List chromatic number itself is finite: with $r>0$ vertices and $r$ colors
per list, greedy choices of distinct colors forbid at most $r-1$ colors at
any vertex. Also, colorability for a smaller uniform list size implies
four-choosability by restricting four-lists to that smaller size. Hence the
bad assignment in the construction implies list chromatic number greater than
four. The set of such finite orders is nonempty, and well-ordering supplies its
minimum. The empty-graph convention cannot affect this minimum or the bound.

**Ruling: pass.** Nonemptiness is a necessary definitional obligation, not an
assumption that can be silently dropped. Here it is discharged openly by the
elementary finite-choice construction already retained in the report. I accept
that as the well-definedness reconstruction authorized by the actual original
commission and the definition rule in `docs/verification.md`, “Report
contract”. It introduces no hypothesis, uninspected theorem, search result,
or change of target, and replaces no deduction excluding any order through
25. Uncolorability of the displayed assignment follows directly from the
definition of list coloring, for every left choice.

This is not a ruling that nonemptiness follows from the palette argument, nor
that its witness was present in the frozen proof. It is a ruling that the
report's fully disclosed elementary check of the least-order notation is
permitted reconstruction, not a substantive repair of a failed lower-bound
argument. The pass for the literal least-order consequence includes this
reported reasoning. No missing proof has been supplied by the grader. No
order-26 example, exact minimum, or general-list-size assertion is inferred.
