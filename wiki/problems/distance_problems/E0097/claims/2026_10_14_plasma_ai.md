***

**Claim.** The answer to [[problems/distance_problems/E0097/_index|Problem 97]]
is no: there is a convex polygon each of whose vertices has at least four other
vertices at distance exactly $1$ from it, so every vertex has four other
vertices equidistant from it and no vertex is of the kind the question asks for.
More generally, for every integer $k\ge2$ there is a convex polygon each of
whose vertices has at least $k$ other vertices at distance $1$, which refutes
the general form of the question, in which some fixed $k$ replaces four. The
claim is a deduction made in this corpus from the accepted disproof of
[[problems/distance_problems/E0096/_index|Problem 96]] by Liam Kruer and Jensen
Kohlmeyer, whose explanation states no consequence for this problem. The
polygon's vertices are a subset of their set with $d=13$, so by their Theorem
1.1 it has at most $N_{13}=2\cdot13^{13}\,2^{13^{14}}$ vertices; the proof gives
no coordinates.

**Scope.** Full: the polygon has no vertex of the kind the question asks for.

**Depends on.**
[[problems/distance_problems/E0096/claims/2026_09_10_kruer_kohlmeyer|The accepted claim on Problem 96]],
where Theorem 1.1 of Kruer and Kohlmeyer supplies, with $d=13$, a finite set in
strictly convex position with more than three unit pairs per point; the
negation of the bound $O(n)$ accepted there, applied with the constant $3$,
supplies such a set as well. The deletion step and the passage to a polygon are
proved below and use nothing else.

**Proof.** For a finite planar set $S$, write $u(S)$ for the number of unordered
pairs of points of $S$ at distance exactly $1$, and $\deg_S(p)$ for the number
of points of $S$ at distance $1$ from a point $p\in S$. Call $S$ in strictly
convex position when no point of $S$ lies in the convex hull of the other
points of $S$.

1. *The input.* Theorem 1.1 of Kruer and Kohlmeyer, as the claim page on
   Problem 96 records it, gives for every integer $d\ge2$ a set $V_d$ in
   strictly convex position with $|V_d|=N_d=2d^d\,2^{d^{d+1}}$ and
   $u(V_d)\ge\frac d4N_d$. With $d=13$ the set $V=V_{13}$ satisfies
   $u(V)\ge\frac{13}4|V|>3|V|$.

2. *Deletion.* Set $V_0=V$. While the current set $V_i$ has a point $p$ with
   $\deg_{V_i}(p)\le3$, choose one such point and set
   $V_{i+1}=V_i\setminus\{p\}$. Removing $p$ removes exactly the $\deg_{V_i}(p)$
   unit pairs that contain $p$ and leaves every other pair, so
   $u(V_{i+1})=u(V_i)-\deg_{V_i}(p)\ge u(V_i)-3$, while $|V_{i+1}|=|V_i|-1$.
   Hence $\varphi(S)=u(S)-3|S|$ satisfies $\varphi(V_{i+1})\ge\varphi(V_i)$, and
   since $\varphi(V_0)>0$, every $V_i$ has $\varphi(V_i)>0$. Each step removes a
   point, so the process stops after at most $|V|$ steps, at a set $W$. Then
   $u(W)>3|W|\ge0$, so $W$ is nonempty, and by the stopping rule every point of
   $W$ has at least four other points of $W$ at distance $1$; in particular
   $|W|\ge5$.

3. *Convex position.* $W$ is a subset of $V$. If a point $p\in W$ lay in the
   convex hull of $W\setminus\{p\}$, it would lie in the convex hull of the
   larger set $V\setminus\{p\}$, against the strictly convex position of $V$.
   So $W$ is in strictly convex position. In particular no three points of $W$
   are collinear, since the middle one would lie in the segment joining the
   other two, so the convex hull $P$ of $W$ is a convex polygon with nonempty
   interior. Every vertex of $P$ is a point of $W$, and every point of $W$ is a
   vertex of $P$: if a point of $W$ were not a vertex, it would be a convex
   combination of the vertices, which are other points of $W$. So the vertices
   of $P$ are exactly the points of $W$.

4. *Conclusion.* Every vertex of $P$ has at least four other vertices at the
   common distance $1$, so no vertex of $P$ has no four other vertices
   equidistant from it, and $P$ refutes the question. The common distance is
   $1$ at every vertex, which the question does not require.

5. *The general form.* For an integer $k\ge2$, take $d=4k-3\ge5$ in Theorem 1.1,
   so that $u(V_d)\ge\frac{4k-3}4|V_d|>(k-1)|V_d|$, and delete points with at
   most $k-1$ others at distance $1$, with $\varphi(S)=u(S)-(k-1)|S|$. The same
   argument leaves a set of at least $k+1\ge3$ points in strictly convex
   position, the vertex set of a convex polygon each of whose vertices has at
   least $k$ other vertices at distance $1$. The case $k=4$ is the polygon
   above.

The negation of the bound $O(n)$ that the accepted Lean proof establishes gives
the input of step 1 directly: applied with the constant $3$, it gives some $n$
for which the most unit pairs among $n$ points in strictly convex position
exceeds $3n$, and since that maximum is taken over counts at most
$\binom n2$, some $n$-point set in strictly convex position has more than $3n$
unit pairs; steps 2 to 4 then apply unchanged.

**Relation to the sources.** The erdosproblems.com remarks on Problems 96 and 97
note that a positive answer to this problem, for four equidistant vertices,
would give by induction at most $3n$ unit pairs among $n$ points in convex
position. The deduction here is the contrapositive of that remark, applied to
the accepted theorem of Problem 96, with the induction written out as the
deletion step above. The converse fails: a counterexample to this problem need
not have many unit pairs, since this problem lets the common distance vary with
the vertex. Kruer and Kohlmeyer's explanation of their disproof of Problem 96
does not apply the remark to their theorem, so the claim is the project's: that
application, written out in full, with the polygon a subset of their set with
$d=13$ and the common distance $1$ at every vertex. The same deduction is
recorded in brief on
[[problems/distance_problems/E0096/claims/2026_09_10_kruer_kohlmeyer|the claim page on Problem 96]].
The deletion step is also stated in general form in the manuscript of Kruer,
Kohlmeyer and Price of 13 September 2026, in its Lemma 4.1 and Remark 4.5, as
[[problems/distance_problems/E0097/claims/2026_09_13_kruer_kohlmeyer_price|its claim page]]
records. That manuscript is unrefereed and a pending claim here, and it derives
its own disproof of this problem from its own construction; this page relies
neither on its lemma nor on its construction.
