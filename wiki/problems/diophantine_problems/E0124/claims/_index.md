# problems/diophantine_problems/E0124/claims

[[problems/diophantine_problems/E0124/_index|..]]

[[problems/diophantine_problems/E0124/claims/1996_01_01_burr_erdos_graham_li|1996_01_01_burr_erdos_graham_li]]: Section 3 of the refereed 1996 Acta Arithmetica paper of Burr, Erdős, Graham
and Li gives the largest integer that is not a sum of distinct
positive-exponent powers from $\{3,4,7\}$, $\{3,5,7,13\}$, $\{3,6,7,13,21\}$
or $\{3,4,5\}$. This settles the second question at $k=1$ for these four sets.

[[problems/diophantine_problems/E0124/claims/2015_07_08_bergelson_simmons|2015_07_08_bergelson_simmons]]: Bergelson and Simmons (Acta Arith. 2017) prove in a refereed paper that the
powers of four disjoint sets of bases, three with reciprocal sums at least 1
and one with gcd 1, are strongly complete. This answers the second question at
tuples that contain such sets.

[[problems/diophantine_problems/E0124/claims/2025_11_29_alexeev|2025_11_29_alexeev]]: Boris Alexeev posted a Lean proof, found by Aristotle from Harmonic, that
every nonnegative integer is a sum of one number with base-$d_i$ digits 0 and
1 for each $i$ when $\sum_i1/(d_i-1)\ge1$. The result is claimed.

[[problems/diophantine_problems/E0124/claims/2026_09_09_fan|2026_09_09_fan]]: Theorem 1.5 of Steve Fan's arXiv preprint (version of 9 September 2026) claims
that the powers of bases split into two parts with reciprocal sums at least 1
and one part with gcd 1 are strongly complete. This answers the second
question at tuples that admit such a split.
