**Claim.** For every $A\subseteq\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$, with $B$ the set of
integers having exactly one representation as an unordered sum $a_1+a_2$ of
elements of $A$ (equal summands allowed), the exception count
$E_A(N)=\lvert\{1,\ldots,N\}\setminus B\rvert$ satisfies
$12000\,E_A(N)>\sqrt N$ for every $N\ge2\cdot10^{24}$. Hence the first
question of [[problems/additive_bases/E0014/_index|Problem 14]] has the answer
yes, $E_A(N)\gg_\epsilon N^{1/2-\epsilon}$ for every $\epsilon>0$, and the
second has the answer no: no $A$ has $E_A(N)=o(N^{1/2})$. Both answers follow
from one finite obstruction, $T^2<1000\,\lvert\{0,\ldots,2T^4\}\setminus
B\rvert$ for every $A$ and every $T\ge10^6$, proved by counting pairs, a
third-moment identity for uniquely represented sums and a generating-function
bound at $z=1-4/T^4$; the two records are one theorem read two ways.

**Formalization.** Conjectures.io's Lean kernel checked both proofs against the
formal-conjectures statements, each target theorem closing by `exact` against
the formal-conjectures type, with the axiom closure the records print; its
verdict rests on one kernel implementation. Two limits of that check are settled
by the corpus's replay. The pinned formal-conjectures commit does not resolve on
GitHub (2026-10-07); Conjectures.io builds it from a public catalog commit and
an audit patch, and the replay reproduced it exactly. The formal-conjectures
definitions `allUniqueSums` and `≫` are restated in each proof file's own
namespace, and the replayed kernel checks that each target closes the
formal-conjectures type itself.

**Reading.** The formal statements take $A$ among all subsets of
$\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$, count unordered pairs with equal summands allowed and count
exceptions in $\{1,\ldots,N\}$; the problem page's Formulation paragraph records
that this is the natural reading of the erdosproblems.com wording and that the
larger range of $A$ only strengthens both answers. In the replayed statements,
`allUniqueSums` counts exactly one unordered pair, equal summands allowed, the
count runs over $\{1,\ldots,N\}$, and `≫` is big-O in the direction the first
question asks, so the first record answers the first question yes and the second
record answers the second no. Neither answer implies the other; both follow from
the uniform bound above. The problem's claim is `answered`: two questions, one
answer each, in opposite directions. The refereed literature,
[[../library/additive_bases/erdos_freud_1991_sums_sidon_sequence/proposition_2|Erdős and Freud's Proposition 2]],
gives a set with at most $2^{3/2}n^{1/2}$ exceptions up to $n$, and the Remark
after it conjectures that this cannot be improved to $o(n^{1/2})$. The uniform
bound proves that Remark for $n\ge2\cdot10^{24}$ and leaves open only the best
constant, between $1/12000$ and $2^{3/2}$.
