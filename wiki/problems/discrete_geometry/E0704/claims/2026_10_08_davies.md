***

**Claim.** Let $G_n$ be the unit distance graph of $\mathbb R^n$; the paper
writes $\chi(\mathbb R^d)$ for $\chi(G_d)$. James Davies, *Measurable
obstructions for unmeasurable colourings*, arXiv:2610.12301v1, posted
2026-10-08 (47 pages, the only version), proves

$$
(2+o(1))^n\le\chi(G_n)\le\Bigl(\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}+o(1)\Bigr)^n,
$$

the lower bound as Theorem 1.4 and the upper bound as Theorem 1.5 (both p. 4,
combined in the display after Theorem 1.5), with $3\sqrt3/2=2.598\ldots$. This
answers yes the second question of
[[problems/discrete_geometry/E0704/_index|Problem 704]]. The abstract prints
the upper base as $3\sqrt3/4$. That is a misprint: Theorem 1.5, the sentence
before it, which gives the value $2.5981\ldots$, and the last line of its
proof (p. 37) all have $3\sqrt3/2$, and $3\sqrt3/4=1.299\ldots$ would lie below
the lower base $2$.

The lower bound is first proved for measurable colorings. Theorem 8.1 (p. 30)
bounds the density of a measurable set avoiding distance $1$ through any
normalized combination $H$ of radial derivatives of the spherical average of
$e^{2\pi i x\cdot\omega}$ with $H\ge-B$, since the autocorrelation of such a set
vanishes on an annulus around the unit sphere; this gives
$\chi^{(m)}(\mathbb R^d)\ge1+1/B$. Section 8 multiplies the average, a Bessel
function $J_{d/2-1}$ up to normalization, by a polynomial that cancels its sign
changes up to $2\nu$, $\nu=d/2-1$, and stays uniformly small beyond (Lemmas 8.2
and 8.3), which gives $B=2^{-d+o(d)}$. The paper's main theorem, Theorem 1.1
(p. 3), states that $\chi(\mathbb R^d)$ equals the measurable chromatic number
$\chi^{(m)}(\mathbb R^d)$ for every $d\ge4$, and so carries the bound to all
colorings. Its proof (Sections 2–7 and Appendices A and B) transfers finite
certificates of the measurable problem, in the completely positive framework of
DeCorte, de Oliveira Filho and Vallentin, to lattice Cayley graphs. The upper
bound (Section 10, pp. 35–37) is a random construction. Balls of radius about
$1/2$ around random periodic centers, trimmed by half-spaces so that the pieces
avoid every distance in $[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]$ with
$\varepsilon=d^{-1/2}$, contain each point with probability at least $p$,
where $1/p\le(3\sqrt3/2+o(1))^d$. The base comes from the maximum $3\sqrt3/4$
of $(1+t)\sqrt{1-t^2}$, which bounds the radius of a cylinder holding the
forbidden centers. $O(d\log d)/p$ independent copies cover a fine grid, and
their $\varepsilon/3$-neighborhoods give a proper Borel coloring. The paper's
statement of AI use says that ChatGPT found the initial proof of Theorem 1.5,
improved the lower-bound arguments of Sections 8 and 9, and gave initial
proofs of parts of Theorem 1.1. Read depth: the statements of Theorems 1.1, 1.4
and 1.5 and the proofs of Theorems 1.4 and 1.5 (Sections 8 and 10), read
through but not checked step by step; the proof of Theorem 1.1 was not read.

**Covers.** The exponential-growth question: $\chi(G_n)$ grows at least
exponentially, with base $2$; the accepted claims of
[[problems/discrete_geometry/E0704/claims/1981_12_01_frankl_wilson|Frankl and Wilson]]
and
[[problems/discrete_geometry/E0704/claims/2000_04_30_raigorodskii|Raigorodskii]]
already settle it. For the estimate, both bases are new against the bounds the
problem page records, Raigorodskii's lower base $1.239\ldots$ and the
Larman–Rogers upper base $3$, which the paper names as the previous bounds
(p. 2). If the limit of $\chi(G_n)^{1/n}$ exists, the two bounds confine it to
$[2,2.598\ldots]$. The upper bound contradicts the guess of Larman and Rogers,
recorded in the erdosproblems.com remarks, that the truth may be
$(2^{3/2}+o(1))^n$: $(3\sqrt3/2)^2=27/4<8=(2^{3/2})^2$, so Theorem 1.5 gives
$\limsup\chi(G_n)^{1/n}\le2.598\ldots<2.828\ldots$. The paper does not mention
that guess; it conjectures instead that one of its two bases is tight,
without saying which. Not covered: the estimate, since the two bases differ,
and the existence of the limit, which the paper does not address.

**Depends on.** No page of this wiki. The lower bound rests on the paper's own
Theorem 1.1, whose proof uses the completely positive characterization of
measurable independence density of DeCorte, de Oliveira Filho and Vallentin,
with a gap in its finite-point converse filled in Appendix A, and a
lattice-point estimate of Magyar for its Theorem 4.1. The upper bound is
self-contained.
