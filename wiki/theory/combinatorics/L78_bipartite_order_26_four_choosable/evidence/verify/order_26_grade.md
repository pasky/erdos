## Mathematical assessment and weakest steps

Restated precisely, for every finite simple bipartite graph $G$ with
$|V(G)|\le26$ and every assignment of a set of exactly four colours to each
vertex, a proper colouring from those lists exists. Colour names are arbitrary;
their union for a fixed assignment is finite. Equal lists at distinct vertices
and equal colours on the same side are allowed. The empty graph and empty
bipartition sides are included. With $n(4)$ the least order of a bipartite graph
whose list chromatic number exceeds four, the consequence is $n(4)\ge27$.

The grader targeted the same consequential interfaces to test the adequacy of
the actual examination: whether an assignment escapes its finite domain,
whether the event bound is logically transferable, and whether its strict
margin survives exact arithmetic. This is grading of one review, not a second
cycle presented as having disjoint attacks.

### Minimum palette, boundary cases and distinctness

The report checks the correct direction of graph completion: a colouring after
padding to 26 vertices and adding cross edges restricts to the original graph.
Thus a bad assignment would stay bad. Empty sides are edgeless and cannot be
counterexamples; side exchange permits $a\le b$, $a+b=26$. The completed
graph is colourable exactly when a palette subset meets every left list and
contains no right list. The report proves both implications using disjoint
colour pools.

For a fixed hypothetical bad $K_{a,b}$, palette cardinalities form a nonempty
set of positive integers bounded by 104, so a least one exists. If two colours
never share a list, identifying them preserves list size and any colouring
lifts: an equality on an edge after lifting would imply an equality before
lifting. The smaller assignment would still be bad, a contradiction. Hence
every colour pair occurs in a list. This yields
$\binom N2\le156$ and
$104\ge N\lceil(N-1)/3\rceil$, excluding $N\ge18$.

All remaining endpoints are actually examined. A palette of size at most six
splits into two parts of size at most three, each met by every four-list.
For $N=7,\ldots,14$, paired middle-layer counts require 26 to be at least,
respectively, $70,70,42,42,33,33,143/5,143/5$, which is impossible. At 16 the
middle layer has exactly one event per subset under badness, but two list
occurrences on one side give a subset with two events. At 15 the two adjacent
layers force $a=b=13$ and equality; two left occurrences again give a double
event. These arguments use occurrences and remain valid for repeated lists.

At $N=17$ only, collapsing duplicate four-sets preserves pair coverage. Each
colour occurs in at least six distinct sets, so their count satisfies
$4q\ge102$ and $q\ge26$. All 26 occurrences must therefore be different,
even across sides. Taking $T=A_1$ now forces a disjoint second left list;
taking $T=P\setminus B_1$ forces a disjoint second right list. The report
does not assume this essential distinctness prematurely. It correctly reaches
two fixed disjoint left four-lists and $2\le a\le13$.

These are present in the frozen proof and independently explained in the
report. The grader found no missing structural lemma supplied only during
review. The attempt to escape through repetitions or an omitted palette case
therefore fails for substantive reasons, not because the checker succeeds.

### Weighted transfer and exact arithmetic

Let $U,V$ be the fixed disjoint four-lists, $W$ the other nine colours and
$\mathcal S$ the subsets meeting both fixed lists. Their two left events
vanish on $\mathcal S$. Badness forces every such subset to be covered by an
event of the remaining $a-2$ left or $26-a$ right lists. Nonnegative finite
weights therefore give

$$
Z\le \sum_{i\ge3}\ell(A_i)+\sum_j r(B_j)
\le (a-2)L+(26-a)R.
$$

No disjointness of events, probabilistic independence, or simultaneous
realizability of maximizing lists is required. Taking maxima over every
four-subset except $U,V$ is a valid enlargement of the remaining inventory.
The eight list types have multiplicities
$126,672,576,432,432,36,72,32$, summing to all 2378 admissible four-subsets.
Thus the numerical calculation has the domain the analytic transfer needs.

The original report independently derives the complement and residual-choice
binomial counts and displays all six group contributions to every miss and
containment row. It does more than quote the source table or compare the
author's two algorithms. Those displayed counts yield

$$
Z=83424,\qquad L=4374,\qquad R=2664,
$$

with attaining lists of types $(1,0,3)$ and $(3,1,0)$. Hence

$$
(a-2)L+(26-a)R=1710a+60516\le82746<83424,
$$

leaving the exact gap 678. The grader's retained direct subset enumeration
checks every displayed group contribution without importing the author's
helper or using either its binomial computation or its subset transforms.
It confirms the original report's arithmetic; that report already contained
the independently reproducible paper leg required by its commission.

### The consequence and routine reconstruction

The report makes four elementary implicit facts explicit: a nonempty set of
integer palette sizes has a minimum; $k$-choosability implies $q$-choosability
for $q\ge k$ by selecting sublists; an $m$-vertex nonempty graph is
$m$-choosable by a finite greedy colouring; and the minimum defining $n(4)$
has a nonempty domain. For the last fact it gives four disjoint left
four-lists and one right four-list for every selection of one colour from each
left list. Completing all cross edges makes every left colouring block its
corresponding right vertex. This is a concrete finite witness to nonemptiness.

These are routine reconstructions permitted by the launched assignment, and
the report explicitly identifies their absence from the source's exposition.
They establish the well-definedness and elementary use of the given catalog
definition without invoking a new theorem, inventory classification, or
unproved source result. They do not replace a failed palette reduction or
weighted inequality. In particular, the nonemptiness construction supplies no
sharp value and does not extend the order-26 attack. The conclusion
$n(4)\ge27$ then follows because no order at most 26 belongs to that
minimum's domain. Treating these explanations as an undisclosed essential
proof repair would misdescribe both their content and the actual commission.

## Qualifications and exact witnesses

all palette subsets and add one unit at $\varnothing$. The total becomes

two fixed left lists, the full upper bound becomes

$$
2+(a-2)(4374+1)+(26-a)2664
=1711a+60516\le82759<83425.

The witness refutes a universal zero-outside-support interpretation of the
closing prose. It does not refute the theorem or the chosen sufficient support
