***

**Claim.** On 2026-05-03 Svyable posted in the thread of
[[problems/additive_bases/E0881/_index|Problem 881]] the manuscript *Infinite
Deletions from Strongly Minimal Additive Bases*, which claims a complete
answer; the post links the ChatGPT conversation that generated the write-up and
a Perplexity conversation used as its referee. For $k=1$ the answer is yes:
removing a sparse infinite subset from a basis of order $1$ leaves a basis of
order $2$ (Proposition 7). For every $k\ge2$ the answer is no (Main Theorem
6): some asymptotic basis $A$ of order $k$, minimal at order $k$ both in
the ordinary sense and under infinite deletions, has $A\setminus B$ not an
asymptotic basis of order $k+1$ for every infinite $B\subset A$, and
$|A\cap[0,x]|=O_k(x^{1/k})$. formal-conjectures states the question for every
order $k$ and every minimal basis (`erdos_881`, the `record` link), with a
basis of order $k$ meaning that all large integers are sums of exactly $k$
elements, so one counterexample at some $k\ge2$ answers it no. The site's
definitions page instead allows sums of at most $k$ elements. The source card
is
[[../library/additive_bases/svyable_2026_infinite_deletions_strongly_minimal_additive_bases/_index|svyable_2026_infinite_deletions_strongly_minimal_additive_bases]].

**Covers.** The variant of the question in which a basis of order $k$ means
that all large integers are sums of exactly $k$ elements, the reading of the
manuscript and of formal-conjectures' `erdos_881`, answered no for every
$k\ge2$ by Main Theorem 6. Not covered: the question as the problem page reads
it, with the site's definition of sums of at most $k$ elements. The
manuscript's set does not contain $0$, and neither its minimality at order $k$
nor its failure at order $k+1$ carries over from exactly $k$ (and $k+1$)
summands to at most $k$ (and $k+1$), as the problem page's Formulation shows,
so the counterexample settles no instance of that question. Only the
manuscript's case $k=1$ (Proposition 7, the answer yes) carries over, since a
basis of order $1$ is the same under both definitions and a basis of order $2$
with exactly two summands is one with at most two; one instance does not
decide the question. The claim's value is `disproved`, the manuscript's answer
in its own reading.

**Standing.** The site's curator wrote the same day that the proof appears to
have been generated entirely by GPT and asked for the use of AI to be
disclosed. A reader posted the same day an AI check, a ChatGPT conversation,
reporting several major gaps. The check finds that the manuscript counts sums
of exactly $k$ elements, so that it could settle at most that reading and not
the site's; that the clause of the witness lemmas placing the witnesses in any
prescribed subinterval (the 'Moreover' clause of Lemma 8, and its analogue for
the booster witnesses) is false as stated, since a witness is a sum of $k$
chosen elements, while the construction relies on it; that the booster step's
scale inequalities fail for $k\ge21$; that the recursion uses the next scale
before choosing it and lacks a uniform estimate for its induction; and that the
finite-booster normal form theorem is not proved. The thread records no reply
to these points. No refutation of the stated result is recorded, so the claim
stays claimed; it is not refereed, reviewed or formalized.
