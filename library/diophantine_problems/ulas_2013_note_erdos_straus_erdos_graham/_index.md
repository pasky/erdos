# Ulas: A note on Erdős–Straus and Erdős–Graham divisibility problems (with an appendix by Andrzej Schinzel)

[[diophantine_problems/_index|..]]

[[diophantine_problems/ulas_2013_note_erdos_straus_erdos_graham/table_2|table_2]]: The least k with (n+1)...(n+k) | (n+k+1)...(n+2k) for n <= 16, and a solution not checked to be least for 17 <= n <= 20: witnesses for Problem 389 at each n from 2 to 21.

[[diophantine_problems/ulas_2013_note_erdos_straus_erdos_graham/table_4|table_4]]: The least k > n with k(k-1)...(k-n) | C(2k, k) for n <= 9, from a search up to 2^30; the rows n = 2 to 9 are Problem 396's least witnesses for its k = 2 to 9, and the rows n = 0 and 1 are not least.

[[diophantine_problems/ulas_2013_note_erdos_straus_erdos_graham/theorem_2_1|theorem_2_1]]: For positive n and k, (n+1)...(n+k) divides (n+k+1)...(n+2k) exactly when every prime p dividing (k+1)...(k+n) has 2s_p(k+n) - s_p(2k+n) - s_p(n) >= 0, where s_p is the base-p digit sum.

[[diophantine_problems/ulas_2013_note_erdos_straus_erdos_graham/theorem_2_2|theorem_2_2]]: Infinitely many k = pq - 1, with p and q primes and 3p/2 < q < 2p, have k + 1 dividing C(2k+1, k), and k - 1 then solves the case n = 2, so Problem 389 has infinitely many solutions at its n = 2 and n = 3.

[[diophantine_problems/ulas_2013_note_erdos_straus_erdos_graham/theorem_2_7|theorem_2_7]]: For every positive n, infinitely many k fail the Erdős–Straus divisibility: k = 2p^m - n, with p a prime above n and at least 5, violates the digit-sum criterion at p.

[[diophantine_problems/ulas_2013_note_erdos_straus_erdos_graham/theorem_3_1|theorem_3_1]]: For integers n >= 0 and k > n, k(k-1)...(k-n) divides C(2k, k) exactly when every prime p dividing that product has 3s_p(k) - s_p(2k) - s_p(k-n-1) >= n + 1, where s_p is the base-p digit sum.

[[diophantine_problems/ulas_2013_note_erdos_straus_erdos_graham/theorem_3_2|theorem_3_2]]: Every k in S_1 gives k + 1 dividing C(2k+2, k+1), so infinitely many k divide C(2k, k); Corollary 3.3 restates this as k dividing the Catalan number C_k for infinitely many k.

[[diophantine_problems/ulas_2013_note_erdos_straus_erdos_graham/theorem_3_4|theorem_3_4]]: For every n >= 0, infinitely many k fail k(k-1)...(k-n) | C(2k, k): each power of 2 that is at least 4 and exceeds n + 1 violates the digit-sum criterion at 2.

[[diophantine_problems/ulas_2013_note_erdos_straus_erdos_graham/theorem_a_1|theorem_a_1]]: For f(x) = ax + b with a > 1 and gcd(a, b) = 1, each n has only finitely many k with f(n+1)...f(n+k) | f(n+k+1)...f(n+2k); the printed choice of prime needs a small repair when n >= 1.

[[diophantine_problems/ulas_2013_note_erdos_straus_erdos_graham/theorem_a_2|theorem_a_2]]: States that for gcd(a, b) = 1 and every n infinitely many k have (ak+b)^n | C(2k, k); the printed proof omits the factor p - 1 of Legendre's formula, so it establishes only the exponent one, and not even that when a = 2 and b > 0.

**The two questions.** Question 1.1 (p. 584), which the paper credits to
Erdős and Straus, asks whether every positive integer $n$ has a positive
integer $k$ with

$$
(n+1)(n+2)\cdots(n+k)\mid(n+k+1)(n+k+2)\cdots(n+2k).
$$

Question 1.2 (p. 584), credited to Erdős and Graham, asks whether every
nonnegative integer $n$ has an integer $k$ with
$k(k-1)\cdots(k-n)\mid\binom{2k}{k}$. The paper takes both from pp. 75 and 76
of
[[number_theory/erdos_1980_old_new_problems_results_combinatorial_number_theory/_index|Erdős and Graham's 1980 problem book]].
It writes $S_n$ for the set of positive $k$ answering Question 1.1 for $n$
and $k_n$ for its least element (p. 585), and $Z_n$ for the set of $k>n$
answering Question 1.2 for $n$ and $z_n$ for its least element (p. 592), so
that $Z_0\supset Z_1\supset\cdots$. Question 1.1 at $n$ is
[[../wiki/problems/diophantine_problems/E0389/_index|Problem 389]] at $n+1$,
and Question 1.2 is
[[../wiki/problems/factorials_binomials/E0396/_index|Problem 396]] with its
two letters exchanged.

**Method.** Legendre's formula turns each divisibility into inequalities
between base-$p$ digit sums, one for each prime $p$ dividing the left side:
[[diophantine_problems/ulas_2013_note_erdos_straus_erdos_graham/theorem_2_1|Theorem 2.1]]
for Question 1.1 and
[[diophantine_problems/ulas_2013_note_erdos_straus_erdos_graham/theorem_3_1|Theorem 3.1]]
for Question 1.2. The searches test these inequalities prime by prime instead
of computing binomial coefficients (pp. 589–590 and 594).

**Question 1.1.**
[[diophantine_problems/ulas_2013_note_erdos_straus_erdos_graham/theorem_2_2|Theorem 2.2]]
shows that $S_1$ is infinite and that $S_1-1\subset S_2$. Propositions 2.4
and 2.5 (pp. 587–588) show that every element of $S_2\setminus(S_1-1)$ is
even and every element of $S_4\setminus(S_3-1)$ is $0$ or $1$ modulo $4$.
[[diophantine_problems/ulas_2013_note_erdos_straus_erdos_graham/theorem_2_7|Theorem 2.7]]
shows that infinitely many $k$ lie outside each $S_n$.
[[diophantine_problems/ulas_2013_note_erdos_straus_erdos_graham/table_2|Table 2]]
reports $k_n$ for $n\le16$ and, for $17\le n\le20$, an element of $S_n$ not
checked to be the least, and Table 3 (p. 591) counts $S_n\cap[1,2^i]$ for
$n\le10$ and $i\le26$. The paper conjectures that every $S_n$ is nonempty
(Conjecture 2.9), that a nonempty $S_n$ is infinite (Conjecture 2.10) and
that $k_{2n-1}-1\in S_{2n}$ (Conjecture 2.11), and asks how
$|S_n\cap[1,x]|$ grows (Question 2.8).

**Question 1.2.**
[[diophantine_problems/ulas_2013_note_erdos_straus_erdos_graham/theorem_3_2|Theorem 3.2]]
shows that $S_1+1\subset Z_0$, so infinitely many $k$ divide $\binom{2k}{k}$,
and Corollary 3.3 (p. 593) that $k$ divides the Catalan number $C_k$ for
infinitely many $k$.
[[diophantine_problems/ulas_2013_note_erdos_straus_erdos_graham/theorem_3_4|Theorem 3.4]]
shows that infinitely many $k$ lie outside each $Z_n$.
[[diophantine_problems/ulas_2013_note_erdos_straus_erdos_graham/table_4|Table 4]]
reports $z_n$ for $n\le9$ from a search up to $2^{30}$; p. 594 lists the 25
elements of $Z_7$ up to $2^{30}$ and the four of them in $Z_8$, and reports
one in $Z_9$; and Table 5 counts $Z_n\cap[1,2^i]$ for $n\le6$. Conjectures
3.5 and 3.6 (p. 595) mirror Conjectures 2.9 and 2.10. Conjecture 3.8
(p. 595) links the two questions: $z_n-(2n+1)\in S_{2n+1}$. The paper used it
to find the rows $17\le n\le20$ of Table 2, and notes that the same shift
fails for other elements of $Z_n$, for instance $20\in Z_0$ while
$19\notin S_1$.

**Section 4 and the appendix.** Section 4 generalizes both questions to a
polynomial $f_A$ with positive leading coefficient. Question 4.1 asks for $k$
with $f_A(n+1)\cdots f_A(n+k)\mid f_A(n+k+1)\cdots f_A(n+2k)$; Questions 4.2,
4.4 and 4.5 ask when $f_A(k)\mid\binom{2k}{k}$ has a solution $k$, or
infinitely many; and Theorem 4.3 (p. 596) gives the digit-sum criterion for
$f_A(k)\mid\binom{2k}{k}$. For $f_A(x)=2x-1$ and $n=1$, Question 4.1 has no
solution (pp. 595–596). Schinzel's appendix takes $f(x)=ax+b$ with
$\gcd(a,b)=1$.
[[diophantine_problems/ulas_2013_note_erdos_straus_erdos_graham/theorem_a_1|Theorem A.1]]
shows that for $a\ge2$ each $n$ has only finitely many solutions of the
analogue of Question 1.1.
[[diophantine_problems/ulas_2013_note_erdos_straus_erdos_graham/theorem_a_2|Theorem A.2]]
states that for every $n$ infinitely many $k$ have
$(ak+b)^n\mid\binom{2k}{k}$; its printed proof establishes only the exponent
one, and in the case $a=2$, $b>0$ not even that.

**Source notes.** The result pages state each point of the print that needs
care; none is an author erratum. Theorems 2.1 and 3.1 are worded as "there
exists an integer $k>n$ such that" one condition holds "if and only if"
another does, with $k$ free in the second, and are proved and used as
criteria for a given $k$. The proof of Theorem 2.2(2) ends with a misprinted
binomial coefficient, and the proof of Theorem 2.7 with a misprinted value and
a choice of prime that needs $p\ge5$; neither affects the theorem. Table 4's
rows for $n=0$ and $n=1$ are not the least elements of $Z_0$ and $Z_1$ under
the paper's own definition. The proof of Theorem A.1 needs its prime above
$((2k+n)a+b)/(a+1)$ rather than $(2ka+b)/(a+1)$, a repair that costs nothing
for fixed $n$. The proof of Theorem A.2 compares $2s_p(k)-s_p(2k)$ with the
exponent without the factor $p-1$ that Legendre's formula and the paper's own
Theorem 4.3 carry.

**Bears on.** [[../wiki/problems/diophantine_problems/E0389/_index|#389]]: the
problem at $n+1$ is Question 1.1 at $n$, and its $n=1$ is trivial, since $k!$
divides $(k+1)\cdots(2k)$. Table 2 (p. 590) reports a witness $k$ for each of
the problem's $n$ from $2$ to $21$, the least one for $n\le17$. Theorem 2.2
(p. 586) gives infinitely many $k$ for the problem's $n=2$ and $n=3$, and
Theorem 2.7 (p. 589) infinitely many failing $k$ for each $n\ge2$.
Conjecture 2.9 (p. 591) is the problem's affirmative answer, and Conjecture
2.10 predicts infinitely many $k$ wherever one exists. Schinzel's Theorem A.1
(p. 597) shows that the analogue along a progression $ax+b$ with $a\ge2$ has
only finitely many $k$ for each $n$. Nothing in the paper reaches the
problem's $n\ge22$.

[[../wiki/problems/factorials_binomials/E0396/_index|#396]]: the problem's $k$
and $n$ are the paper's $n$ and $k$. Table 4 (p. 593) reports, from a search
up to $2^{30}$, the least witness for each of the problem's $k$ from $2$ to
$9$. These eight values are the terms $a(2)$ to $a(9)$ of the OEIS entry
A375077 that the problem's claim pages record and credit to contributors from
2024 to 2026; the paper printed them in 2013. Its rows for $k=0$ and $k=1$
are not least under the paper's definition, as
[[diophantine_problems/ulas_2013_note_erdos_straus_erdos_graham/table_4|the Table 4 page]]
explains. Theorem 3.2 (p. 592) gives infinitely many $n$ for $k=0$, Theorem
3.4 (p. 593) infinitely many failing $n$ for each $k$, and Conjecture 3.5
(p. 595) is the problem's affirmative answer for $k\ge1$. Schinzel's Theorem
A.2 (p. 597) concerns a power of one linear factor and settles no instance
with $k\ge1$.
