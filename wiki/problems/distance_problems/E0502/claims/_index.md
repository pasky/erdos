# problems/distance_problems/E0502/claims

[[problems/distance_problems/E0502/_index|..]]

[[problems/distance_problems/E0502/claims/1983_06_01_bannai_bannai_stanton|1983_06_01_bannai_bannai_stanton]]: Bannai, Bannai and Stanton prove that a set in $\mathbb R^n$ whose points
determine only two distinct distances has at most $\binom{n+2}{2}$ points.
More generally, an $s$-distance set has at most $\binom{n+s}{s}$ points.

[[problems/distance_problems/E0502/claims/1997_02_01_lisonek|1997_02_01_lisonek]]: Correct, but answers the erdosproblems.com wording (the exact maximum, for
$n\le8$), not the modified Statement (the asymptotic behavior of $M_2(n)$), so
it does not count toward the problem's standing. Lisoněk (1997) determines the
largest two-distance sets in $\mathbb{R}^n$ for every $n\le8$: 3, 5, 6, 10,
16, 27, 29 and 45 points, the last attaining the Bannai–Bannai–Stanton bound.

[[problems/distance_problems/E0502/claims/2025_08_09_alweiss|2025_08_09_alweiss]]: The erdosproblems.com curator credits Ryan Alweiss with this construction: the
points $e_i+e_j$ lie on a hyperplane and form a two-distance set of
$\binom{n+1}{2}$ points in $\mathbb R^n$, which gives the lower bound. It is
accepted on the curator's credit.
